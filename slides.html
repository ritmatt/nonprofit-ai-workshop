Stage 1 — Explore
Your Safe Space to Learn

✓ Great for learning:

  • Public information about your sector or issue area
  • Drafting general communications (no donor names)
  • Brainstorming program ideas or event formats
  • Researching grant funders and best practices
  • Summarizing publicly available reports or articles

✗ Keep this out of free AI tools:

  • Donor names, contact info, or giving history
  • Client or beneficiary information of any kind
  • Financial data, budgets, or bank information
  • Staff records or personnel matters
  • Any personally identifiable information (PII)
The Goal of ExploreBuild comfort. Identify 2–3 time-saving use cases that work for you. Once you've done that, you're ready for Protect.

Stage 2 — Protect
Most Small Nonprofits Have Data Chaos

The problem:

  • Shared email accounts (info@, donations@) with years of sensitive correspondence
  • Files scattered across personal computers, Dropbox, Google Drive, and that one person's desktop
  • Shared logins instead of individual user accounts
  • No clear system for what lives where — or who has access

You can't create meaningful AI data policies if you don't know where your data actually is.

The good news: AI can help fix this

  • Audit what systems and accounts you're currently using
  • Create a file organization structure that makes sense for your team
  • Draft policies for where different types of information should live
  • Build a step-by-step migration plan to consolidate and clean up
Before Team DeploymentIndividual user accounts · Defined folders for sensitive data · Everyone knows what's confidential

Stage 2 — Protect
Simple AI Usage Guidelines
✓ Okay to use with free AI tools
Public information · General research · Drafts with no names or private details · Brainstorming · Summarizing public documents
✗ Never in public AI tools
Donor lists or giving history · Beneficiary / client names · Staff records or HR matters · Financial accounts or budgets · Any PII (personally identifiable information)
For sensitive work, use paid tools
Microsoft Copilot (stays in your M365 tenant) · ChatGPT Plus or Team plans · Claude Pro or Team plans

Paid accounts generally don't use your data to train their models.
Keep It SimpleA one-page policy is enough to start. Don't wait for the perfect policy before getting your team started.

Getting started
Choosing Your Starting Tool
ChatGPT
For general learning
Most widely used. Huge library of tutorials, YouTube videos, and community resources. Free tier is solid. Strong choice if you're starting from scratch.
Claude
For writing and analysis
Excellent for longer writing, nuanced analysis, and complex reasoning. Strong at editing, summarizing, and working through ambiguous problems. Also handles spreadsheets well.
Microsoft Copilot
If you use Microsoft 365
Works inside Word, Outlook, Teams, and Excel. Stays within your Microsoft tenant — better privacy for organizational data. May already be included in your subscription.
Google Gemini
If you use Google Workspace
Integrates with Gmail, Docs, and Sheets. Understands your Google Drive files. Natural fit if your team already lives in Google.

Pick one. Try it for two weeks. The best tool is the one you'll actually use.

Avoid these
Common Mistakes — and How to Fix Them
❌ Too Vague
"Write a fundraising email."
✓ Specific & Contextual
"Write a 150-word fundraising email for our annual gala targeting lapsed donors, emphasizing our new scholarship program."
❌ No Context
"Help me write a grant proposal."
✓ Rich Context
"We're a 5-person tutoring nonprofit in Gwinnett County serving 200 students. Help me write the program description section of a grant to [Funder Name], which prioritizes measurable outcomes."
❌ Accept First Draft
If it's 70% right, start over or just use it anyway.
✓ Iterate
"Good start. Make it shorter, more personal, and replace the second paragraph with something focused on student success stories."
Always fact-check AI outputAI can and does make things up — especially statistics, dates, program details, and regulatory claims. Never publish AI-generated facts without verification.
10:16 AM
Nonprofit-specific
Pitfalls Unique to Your Sector
  • Don't let AI write your mission statement or core messaging. That's identity work — it requires your community's voice, not a language model's best guess.
  • Be careful with grant proposals. AI can help with structure and editing, but heavy AI content without heavy human editing raises flags with funders. Use it as a tool, not a ghostwriter.
  • Donor communications need to feel human. Authenticity is your competitive advantage over large organizations. Use AI to draft, but make every message sound like it came from you.
  • Never use AI for decisions involving vulnerable populations without human oversight. Program eligibility, case management, crisis response — these require human judgment, full stop.

Workshop · Step 2
Try a Prompt

Use one of these starters:

For writing/communications
"I run a nonprofit that [brief description]. Write me [specific thing] for [specific audience]. Tone should be [warm/professional/urgent]. Length: about [X] words."
For decision-making
"I'm the [role] of [org type]. I'm trying to decide [decision]. The options are [A] and [B]. Help me think through the tradeoffs and what questions I should be asking."
For research
"What are best practices for [topic] among small nonprofits? I'm specifically interested in [angle]. Give me 5 concrete examples with brief explanations."
For summarizing
"Here are my notes from [meeting/document]. Please: (1) summarize the key decisions, (2) list all action items with owners, (3) flag any unresolved questions."

Keys to a good prompt:

  • Give context: Who you are, what your org does, who the audience is
  • Be specific: Length, tone, format, what to include or avoid
  • Iterate: If it's 70% right, ask AI to refine it — don't start over
  • Ask follow-ups: "Make it shorter." "Change the tone." "Add a CTA."
⏱ 10–12 minutes
